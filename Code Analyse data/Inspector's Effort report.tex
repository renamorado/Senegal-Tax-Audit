\subsection{Regression Analysis - Description of Approach}

\textbf{Objective.} Using predicted evasion estimates generated by the Random Forest model, we test whether execution of algorithm-assigned cases depends on whether the "first-in-line" for execution algorithm case(s) on a given list are high- vs. low-potential. The "list" unit differs by audit type: for desk audits it is defined at the inspector $\times$ selection-year level, while for full audits it is defined at the bureau (detailed) $\times$ selection-year level. We compare execution rates of algorithm cases across these lists.

\textbf{Steps.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Load predicted evasion estimates data which includes estimates for selected audits, and restrict by audit type (full or desk audit).} 
    We use the dataset containing predicted evasion (\texttt{yhatrf}) and execution outcomes, and split the analysis by audit type (\texttt{x2==0} desk; \texttt{x2==1} full).

    \item \textbf{Construct predicted-evasion quantile groups (audit-type specific).}
    We compute quintiles of predicted evasion \texttt{yhatrf} in the following way:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Desk audits:} quintiles within \texttt{verificateur\_selection $\times$ selectionyear}.
        \item \textbf{Full audits:} quintiles within \texttt{bureau\_detailed $\times$ selectionyear}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Restrict to algorithm-assigned cases.}
    We keep only algorithm-selected audits (\texttt{algorithm==1}) so that all outcomes and tags refer to the execution of algorithm-assigned cases.

    \item \textbf{Define "top of the list" using execution sequence among algorithm cases.}
    Within each list, we order algorithm cases by the sequencing variable (\texttt{sequencing}) to define early algorithm cases (e.g., Top 1/Top 2, first 10\% or first 20\% of the algorithm sequence).

    \item \textbf{Tag lists based on the potential of first-in-line algorithm cases.}
    For each list, we create indicators for whether an first-in-line algorithm case is:
    (i) \textbf{high-potential} (top quintile of \texttt{yhatrf}, \texttt{yhatrf\_q5==5}), or 
    (ii) \textbf{low-potential} (bottom quintile, \texttt{yhatrf\_q5==1}).
    This generates list-level groups such as "high-potential-first" vs "low-potential-first".

    \item \textbf{Collapse to the list level and compute execution rates.}
    We collapse the data to the list level and compute the mean execution rate of assigned algorithm cases within each list (mean of \texttt{y2} among \texttt{algorithm==1}).

    \item \textbf{Compare execution outcomes across list groups.}
    We present (i) boxplots of list-level algorithm execution rates (unweighted and weighted by the number of assigned algorithm cases), and (ii) regression estimates comparing execution rates across "high-potential-first" and "low-potential-first" lists (with fixed effects varying by audit type).
\end{enumerate}

\item Notes: This table reports regression estimates of how the presence of high- or low-predicted-evasion (high-/low-potential) cases at the top of an algorithm list is associated with the execution rate of algorithm-assigned audits. The unit of observation is a bureau-by-selection-year list $(b,t)$. The dependent variable is $y_{bt}$, the execution rate of algorithm-selected cases in bureau $b$ and selection year $t$ (executed algorithm-selected cases divided by assigned algorithm-selected cases). To classify the audit case potential we use the  estimated  predicted evasion from the Random Forest model and partitioned it into quintiles within tax office $\times$ selection year; a  "high" predicted evasion corresponds to the top quintile and "low"' predicted evasion to the bottom quintile. Panel A presents the results of the  discrete specification of an OLS regression through the following equation:  $y_{bt}=\alpha+\beta_H\,\text{HighTop}_{bt}+\beta_L\,\text{LowTop}_{bt}+\delta_t+\mu_b+\varepsilon_{bt}$, where $\text{HighTop}_{bt}=1$ if the top of the ranking contains a high-predicted-evasion case under the relevant top-of-list definition (Top 2, first 10\% of the sequence, first 20\% of the sequence), and 0 otherwise; $\text{LowTop}_{bt}$ is defined analogously for low-predicted-evasion cases. Panel B presents the results of an alternative specification using a continuous measure of high potential cases, by estimating: $y_{bt}=\alpha+\theta\,\text{ShareHighTop}_{bt}+\delta_t+\mu_b+\varepsilon_{bt}$, where $\text{ShareHighTop}_{bt}$ is the share of high-predicted-evasion cases at the top of the list (same top-of-list definitions as in Panel A). Columns labeled No FE omit fixed effects, Selection Year FE include $\delta_t$ only, and Bureau FE include $\mu_b$ only. Standard errors are robust. Stars denote significance at $^{*}p<0.10$, $^{**}p<0.05$, $^{***}p<0.01$.
\end{tablenotes}

\subsection{Approach 2: Comparing previous year performance with next year's execution rate }
In this subsection we compare the inspectors' performance between two periods based on selection year. We proceed to plot per selection method, i) the share of executed cases from  year $t$ vrs $t+1$, and ii) the share of void audits in $t$ versus the share of execution of assigned audits in $t+1$.  

\begin{itemize}
    \item{Sample: Only desk - audit inspectors that are observable for at least two periods. 
    Furthermore, we divide the sample  between inspectors that changed tax office in the next period and those that remained in the same tax office.}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Definition of shares}

Let Alg $=$ algorithm  + random cases.
Let $N$ denote the number of cases per inspector $i$ at (selection) year $t$. Then, 

\textbf{Share of void cases:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item{$\text{Share of all void cases}_{it} = \frac{N^\text{Total Void}_{it}}{N^{\text{Total Executed}}_{it}}$}

    \item{$\text{Share of void alg cases}_{it} = \frac{N^\text{Void Alg}_{it}}{N^{\text{Total Alg Executed}}_{it}}$}
    
     \item{$\text{Share of void Inspector cases}_{it} = \frac{N^\text{Void Inspector}_{it}}{N^{\text{Total Inspector Executed}}_{it}}$}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Share of executed cases}
\begin{itemize}
    \item{$\text{Share of all executed cases}_{it} = \frac{N^\text{Total Executed}_{it}}{N^{\text{Total Assigned}}_{it}}$}

     \item{$\text{Share of executed Alg cases}_{it} = \frac{N^\text{Executed Alg}_{it}}{N^{\text{Total Alg Assigned}}_{it}}$}

     \item{$\text{Share of executed Inspector cases}_{it} = \frac{N^\text{Executed Inspector}_{it}}{N^{\text{Total Inspector Assigned}}_{it}}$}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Approach 3: Top-listed high-potential algorithm-selected audits}

\textbf{Objective.} Using predicted evasion estimates generated by the Random Forest model, we test whether execution of algorithm-assigned cases depends on whether the "first-in-line" for execution algorithm case(s) on a given list are high- vs. low-potential. The "list" unit differs by audit type: for desk audits it is defined at the inspector $\times$ selection-year level, while for full audits it is defined at the bureau (detailed) $\times$ selection-year level. We compare execution rates of algorithm cases across these lists.

\textbf{Steps.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Load predicted evasion estimates data which includes estimates for selected audits, and restrict by audit type (full or desk audit).} 
    We use the dataset containing predicted evasion (\texttt{yhatrf}) and execution outcomes, and split the analysis by audit type (\texttt{x2==0} desk; \texttt{x2==1} full).

    \item \textbf{Construct predicted-evasion quantile groups (audit-type specific).}
    We compute quintiles of predicted evasion \texttt{yhatrf} in the following way:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Desk audits:} quintiles within \texttt{verificateur\_selection $\times$ selectionyear}.
        \item \textbf{Full audits:} quintiles within \texttt{bureau\_detailed $\times$ selectionyear}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Restrict to algorithm-assigned cases.}
    We keep only algorithm-selected audits (\texttt{algorithm==1}) so that all outcomes and tags refer to the execution of algorithm-assigned cases.

    \item \textbf{Define "top of the list" using execution sequence among algorithm cases.}
    Within each list, we order algorithm cases by the sequencing variable (\texttt{sequencing}) to define early algorithm cases (e.g., Top 1/Top 2, first 10\% or first 20\% of the algorithm sequence).

    \item \textbf{Tag lists based on the potential of first-in-line algorithm cases.}
    For each list, we create indicators for whether an first-in-line algorithm case is:
    (i) \textbf{high-potential} (top quintile of \texttt{yhatrf}, \texttt{yhatrf\_q5==5}), or 
    (ii) \textbf{low-potential} (bottom quintile, \texttt{yhatrf\_q5==1}).
    This generates list-level groups such as "high-potential-first" vs "low-potential-first".

    \item \textbf{Collapse to the list level and compute execution rates.}
    We collapse the data to the list level and compute the mean execution rate of assigned algorithm cases within each list (mean of \texttt{y2} among \texttt{algorithm==1}).

    \item \textbf{Compare execution outcomes across list groups.}
    We present (i) boxplots of list-level algorithm execution rates (unweighted and weighted by the number of assigned algorithm cases), and (ii) regression estimates comparing execution rates across "high-potential-first" and "low-potential-first" lists (with fixed effects varying by audit type).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Measures of inspector effort by predicted evasion}

\subsection{Whole Sample Bins}
The bin-plot analysis uses the Random Forest predicted evasion score \texttt{yhatrf}, generated from the replication R pipeline and stored in \texttt{fullaudits\_predicted.dta} / \texttt{deskaudits\_predicted.dta}. The model is trained on executed audits with observed realized evasion (\texttt{y2==1} and non-missing \texttt{y4}) and then applied to the full selected sample.

\begin{itemize}
\item The two prediction files (\texttt{fullaudits\_predicted.dta} and \texttt{deskaudits\_predicted.dta}) are merged, and all steps are run separately by audit type (\texttt{x2==1} full, \texttt{x2==0} desk).
\item Admin duration is cleaned by setting \texttt{y19=.} when \texttt{y19==0}.
\item Firm size is constructed as mean turnover over 2014--2020 (\texttt{turnover\_mean}).
\item For each x-variable (\texttt{yhatrf}, \texttt{turnover\_mean}), bins are created on the pooled selected sample using quantile bins (quintiles and deciles) and fixed-width bins (\texttt{w1/w2/w4/w5}).
 Bin assignment is done before \texttt{keep if y2==1}, so executed-only filtering is applied later for number of inspectors and duration outcomes.
\item For number of inspectors and duration plots, only executed audits are kept (\texttt{y2==1}), data are collapsed by method and bin for \texttt{numberagents}, \texttt{y19}, \texttt{q30}, and \texttt{y8}, and 95\% confidence intervals are computed using outcome-specific non-missing counts.
\item Algorithm and inspector series are plotted with confidence bands, and one PDF is exported per outcome, x-variable, audit type, bin definition, and bin scope.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Regression equation and notation}

Our empirical exercise studies whether larger discrepancies between the notified and confirmed audit amounts are associated with taxpayer-reported perceptions of the audit process. Concretely, for each taxpayer-survey outcome, we estimate the following model standard OLS regression:
\[
y_{ig}
=
\beta_0
+ \beta_1 W_i
+ \beta_2 \text{Algorithm}_i
+ \beta_3 \text{Overlap}_i
+ \beta_4 \text{Random}_i
+ \beta_5 \text{Safeties}_i
+ \beta_6 \text{Horsprogramme}_i
+ \gamma_g
+ \varepsilon_{ig},
\]
where \(y_{ig}\) is the taxpayer-survey outcome for firm-audit observation \(i\), and \(\gamma_g\) denotes the relevant fixed effects. The unit of observation is a firm-audit observation in the merged administrative audit and taxpayer-survey data, so a given firm may appear more than once if it is associated with multiple audit cases.

Our main discrepancy regressor is
\[
W_i
=
\max\left\{
\frac{\text{Notification}_i-\text{Confirmation}_i}{\text{Notification}_i},
\,0
\right\},
\]
defined for observations with strictly positive notified and confirmed amounts and an executed audit. Thus, \(W_i=0\) both when there is no positive discrepancy and when the confirmed amount is at least as large as the notified amount.

The indicators \(\text{Algorithm}_i\), \(\text{Overlap}_i\), \(\text{Random}_i\), \(\text{Safeties}_i\), and \(\text{Horsprogramme}_i\) capture the audit-case classification. The dummy for random selection is applicable only to desk-audit specifications. The coefficient of interest is \(\beta_1\), which captures the association between the capped discrepancy measure and the taxpayer-reported outcome.

We estimate this equation separately by panel and audit type. Panel A restricts the sample to firms that report having been audited recently,
\[
\texttt{q1 != . \& selfreported\_audit == 1},
\]
while Panel B includes all surveyed firms with non-missing \texttt{q1},
\[
\texttt{q1 != .}.
\]
For audit type, we estimate the specification separately for full audits (\texttt{x2 == 1}), desk audits (\texttt{x2 == 0}), and the pooled sample with no restriction on \texttt{x2}.

The outcomes are the taxpayer-survey indices
\[
\texttt{index\_efficiency}, \quad \texttt{index\_corruption},
\]
and the individual survey questions
\[
\texttt{q34}, \quad \texttt{q35}, \quad \texttt{q32}, \quad \texttt{q42}.
\]
For index outcomes, the fixed effects \(\gamma_g\) correspond to \texttt{selectionyear} \(\times\) \texttt{center}. For question-level outcomes, \(\gamma_g\) corresponds to \texttt{inspectorclusteryear}. We use robust standard errors to perform inference.

In robustness checks, we also replace \(W_i\) with indicator versions:
\[
W_i^{\neq 0}=\mathbf{1}\{W_i\neq 0\},
\qquad
W_i^{>\mathrm{med}}=\mathbf{1}\{W_i>\widetilde{W}\},
\qquad
W_i^{\ge q75}=\mathbf{1}\{W_i\ge\widetilde{W}_{75}\},
\]
where \(\widetilde{W}\) is the pooled median and \(\widetilde{W}_{75}\) is the pooled 75th percentile of non-missing \(W_i\) in the relevant regression sample.
